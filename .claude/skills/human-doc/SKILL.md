# human-doc

You are about to write for a human reader whose attention, memory, and patience are the scarce resources. Your own production cost is irrelevant; spend it freely. The piece is done when it is cheap *to absorb*, not when it is complete.

An essay adds one thing to that: it has to be readable straight through, once, by someone who did not ask for it. No section-hopping, no reference-chasing, no second pass to understand the first. Everything below serves that.

## Rules

1. **No term used as a variable.** If you coin or borrow a handle, unpack it in the same sentence, every time distance makes the reader's memory doubtful. Repetition is a feature. Never make the reader look something up that you could have restated. A named concept — *resistance*, *trivialization* — gets its definition re-attached each time it reappears after a few paragraphs' gap.

2. **No long-range references.** "As shown above," "recall that," "see the earlier section" — banned. If the reader needs it here, put it here, in full. In an essay this is absolute: there is no scrolling back.

3. **Worked example before abstraction.** Every general claim gets a concrete instance *first*. If you cannot produce the instance, flag the claim as unsupported rather than stating it smoothly. At essay scale this also shapes the opening: start inside a specific situation the reader can picture, and let the thesis arrive out of it. A concept introduced before its instance has already lost half the readers.

4. **Deliberate seams.** A seam is a fork where the choice was genuinely open: more than one branch could plausibly reach the goal, and the choice rests on judgment, risk, or information nobody has yet. These get discussed in the text at the length they deserve — a sentence up to a full paragraph: name the branches, the reason for the choice, the risk accepted, and where possible the observation or experiment that would settle the question and trigger a revisit. Phrase them so the reader can grab them: "this choice assumes X; if you don't buy X, here is what changes." A text with no visible joints induces deference. Do not sand every surface.

   **And say when you chose not to open one.** *You* pick which steps become seams, and that pick is an exercise of judgment the reader cannot audit — a seam offered is not a seam found. So where you left a load-bearing step closed because opening it would cost more than it is worth, name it in one line and let the reader decide: "I did not open X; ask if you want it." Otherwise the rule quietly hands the reader a curated set of things they are permitted to doubt, which is the deference it exists to prevent, arriving through the door marked exit. In a published essay, where you cannot be asked, the line becomes a stated assumption the reader is free to reject.

5. **Ban the tells.** Compression pride, elegant density, conclusions that click shut — and the mirror tell, fork-enumeration: assertions arriving with their rejected alternative attached ("it is not this, but that"). Both are machine cost-structure leaking through. If a paragraph feels satisfyingly final, check whether it closed a door the reader might have wanted open; if it weighs an alternative, check whether the decision was ever open at all (rule 7). Endings are where this bites hardest: an essay may land, but it must not seal.

6. **The reader's language.** If you know who the reader is, write in their register and vocabulary, not yours. If you don't know, ask before writing. For a public essay there is no single reader to ask, so name the intended one explicitly before drafting — "someone who works with AI in any field, no technical background assumed" — and hold the whole piece to that person's vocabulary. Project jargon gets translated out, even when translating costs a paragraph.

7. **One trail; weigh only real forks.** Picture the essay as a hiking trail toward a stated goal. At most forks nothing is open: one path leads to Munich, the other to Berlin, and the goal is Munich — so write "we take the fork toward Munich" and walk on. The same holds for steps decided by an earlier commitment or an established process. Weighing such forks anyway is discussion spent where no decision exists; spend discussion only at real forks, which are the seams of rule 4. Closed branches the reader still has a right to see go into one enumeration-register section at the end. Honest caveat: this rule produces the trail's *form*, not the judgment that normally certifies it — the omissions are only as trustworthy as the review the text survived. Form is evidence of the conditions that usually produce it, and evidence can be forged; writing in trail form does not make the content true, and claims no authority.

## Procedure

1. Ask (or determine from context): who is the reader, and what should they be able to *do or say* after reading? Write both down in one line before drafting.
2. Draft under the rules above. One trail from the opening situation to the goal.
3. Close with the forks section — the branches the essay declined, each with its reasons on record (rule 7). This is the one place enumeration register is correct.
4. Where the text was written with an AI and carries a human signature, say so in the text, and say what each side supplied. Provenance is part of what makes the piece readable as a human document rather than a machine artifact.
5. Self-check against each rule — not for polish, but for violations. List any rule you knowingly bent and why, at the end, visibly.
6. Do NOT run this skill on specs, plans, or skill files, including this one. Self-application dissolves content. If asked to, decline and say why.

## What this skill is not

Not summarization (that optimizes *your* cost). Not simplification (the content stays whole; the *access* gets cheap). Not persuasion (seams exist so the reader can disagree).
